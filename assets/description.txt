This haiku suggests a feeling of disconnection from the natural world and from any fixed origin. The phrase "no root in nature" implies something that lacks grounding, belonging, or a true place in the order of things. The next line, "Every history which is," feels fragmented and reflective, as if all human stories are incomplete or uncertain. The final phrase, "heaven and earth to," opens outward into a vast, unfinished space, suggesting a connection between the spiritual and the earthly, but one that remains unresolved. Overall, the haiku feels philosophical and elusive, exploring identity, existence, and the tension between nature, history, and the cosmos.
